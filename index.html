About

One grower's bench, shared

Smokin' Bonsai is my bench of tropical and subtropical trees, styled and cared for in the Florida heat. I started with a single Portulacaria in 2022, and nearly everything I know came from trial, error and generous people in the bonsai community.

This page is my way of passing that along. Every grower was a beginner once, and the best thing anyone did for me was answer my questions. So ask. The advice here is always free.

Start here

Your first steps

01

Pick a forgiving tree

Start with something that loves Florida: a ficus (like Green Island or retusa), a Portulacaria (dwarf jade) or a bougainvillea. They grow fast, bounce back from mistakes and live outside all year.

02

Buy for the trunk

Look for nursery stock with a thick, interesting trunk and healthy roots, not a finished-looking tree. Branches grow in a season; trunks take years. Some of my favorite trees started as cheap big-box finds.

03

Get a few basic tools

A concave cutter, sharp shears, a root hook or chopstick and some aluminum wire will cover your first year. Add more only when you know you need them.

04

Learn to water

Most first trees die from watering, not styling. Use a fast-draining soil, check it every day, and water thoroughly when the top starts to dry. The care guide has the details.

05

Let it grow, then shape

Spend the first season getting the tree healthy. Then start pruning and wiring a little at a time. Doing too much too soon is the most common beginner mistake.

06

Find your people

Join a local club like the Bonsai Society of Brevard, visit a local bonsai nursery like Penjing Bonsai Garden in Malabar, go to workshops and shows, and ask questions. One afternoon with experienced growers will teach you more than a month of videos.

Care guide

The basics, Florida style

Watering

Water when the top of the soil just starts to dry, then soak until water runs from the drainage holes. In a Florida summer that can be once or twice a day. Never water on a schedule alone; check the soil with a finger or a chopstick.

Sun and placement

Most trees here want bright outdoor light. Tropicals like ficus, schefflera and bougainvillea love sun, but give young or freshly repotted trees afternoon shade in midsummer. Indoors is a short visit, not a home.

Soil and repotting

Use a fast-draining mix (akadama, pumice, lava or calcined clay with a little bark). Repot tropicals in late spring to early summer when they're growing hard, usually every two to three years. Trim no more than about a third of the roots.

Fertilizing

Feed regularly through the growing season with a balanced fertilizer at half strength, or solid organic cakes on the soil. Ease off in the coolest weeks and skip feeding right after a repot until new growth shows.

Pruning and wiring

Let a branch run, then cut back to two or three leaves to build ramification. Wire when the wood is flexible, and check it weekly. In Florida growth is fast, so wire can bite in within weeks. Remove it before it scars.

Portulacaria (dwarf jade)

Portulacaria grows fast, so it's built mostly by clip-and-grow, not heavy wire. Trim new shoots often, cutting just above the first pair of leaves; each cut splits into two branches. Don't let shoots run for a month, or the leaves come back big.

Pots and soil. Grow it in a big container while you're building the trunk; a bonsai pot slows it down. Move to a shallow pot once the structure is there. Use a fast-draining, sifted mix (calcined clay plus a coarse mix). Repot every two to four years in late spring, and hold off watering for a few days after heavy root work so the cuts can dry.

Water and feeding. In our hot, dry April and May it can take water every day. In the rainy season let the rain do most of it and make sure pots drain fast, because a jade that sits wet will rot. Wrinkled, shriveled leaves mean it's thirsty. Jades in development like heavy feeding in spring and summer: slow-release pellets on top plus a liquid feed about every four weeks. Nothing in winter.

Big cuts and defoliation. Cut outside the branch collar and let it die back to the next node. Don't seal jade cuts with paste; it holds moisture and can cause rot. Defoliate only healthy, well-rooted trees, and only from April to July. Feed right after, and smaller leaves come back in about a month. Never defoliate and repot at the same time.

Wiring. Let it go dry for a week or more first so branches bend instead of snapping, which is easiest in our dry season. Use soft aluminum wire, two thin wires instead of one thick one, wrapped at about 45 degrees. Leaves drop when you wire; they come back. Leave wire on six to twelve months but watch for bite, and cut it off in pieces. Never unwind it.

Cold. Bring it in before nights drop into the low 40s.

Jade tips adapted from Gilbert Cantu of Little Jade Bonsai, who grows in a climate a lot like ours.

Pests and problems

Watch for scale, mealybugs, spider mites and thrips, especially on the undersides of leaves. A strong rinse, insecticidal soap or horticultural oil handles most of it. Yellowing leaves usually point to watering, not food.

Florida seasons

Summer heat and daily storms mean more water and good drainage. Secure trees before tropical storms or bring them in. On rare frost nights, move tropicals into a garage or cover them; most don't like going below about 40°F.

Fall in Florida

October and November bring uneven rain, so check pots every day instead of watering on a schedule, and keep drainage holes clear. Keep feeding lightly with a balanced fertilizer while trees are still growing, since heavy rain washes nutrients out. Look over new leaves and leaf undersides for aphids, scale and fungus, and treat early. Keep pinching new shoots and finish any wiring before winter. Hold off repotting unless a pot has stopped draining. Take last cuttings and separate air layers before the first cold snap.

Adapted from the Bonsai Society of Brevard's fall care notes.

Florida care calendar

What to do this month

Tropicals grow nearly all year here, and hurricane season runs June through November. This month is highlighted.

Cool and dry

January

The coolest month. Watch the forecast and move tropicals into a garage or cover them on nights below about 40°F. Water less often, since trees dry out slowly. Feed lightly or not at all. Good month to wire junipers and other conifers, clean benches and check for scale.

Cool and dry

February

Still cool, so keep cold-night protection ready. Temperate trees like elms, maples and junipers start to wake; repot them as buds swell. Start feeding temperate trees once new growth appears.

Spring flush

March

The spring flush starts. Finish repotting temperate trees. Begin regular feeding across the bench. Pests wake up too, so check leaf undersides weekly.

Dry and warming

April

The dry season heats up, so watering picks up fast, often daily. Tropicals push hard; let shoots run, then cut back. Bougainvillea comes into color. The dry stretch is the easiest time to wire jades, since they bend best a little dry.

Hot and dry

May

Usually the hottest, driest stretch before the rains. Water at least daily and check pots in the afternoon. Late May opens the repotting window for tropicals like ficus, schefflera and bougainvillea. Healthy ficus and Portulacaria can be defoliated now.

Rainy season

June

Rainy season starts, and so does hurricane season on June 1. Prime time to repot and wire tropicals. Watch for fungus with the daily storms, and make a plan for where trees go if a storm comes.

Rainy season

July

Peak growth. Cut back often to build branching, and check wire every week, because it bites in quickly. Give weaker or freshly repotted trees some afternoon shade.

Rainy season

August

Heat and storms. Water on the dry days, keep feeding, and keep up with pruning. Air layers started in spring are often ready to separate. Stay ready to secure trees ahead of storms.

Rainy season

September

Peak of hurricane season. Keep trees secured or ready to move. Finish any major work on tropicals this month so they recover before it cools.

Season change

October

Rains ease and the heat breaks. Slow down heavy pruning on tropicals and keep feeding lightly. A good month to style and wire junipers.

Cool and dry

November

The dry season returns and nights cool. Water less often, but don't let pots dry out completely. Feed lightly, tidy up and treat any pests before winter.

Cool and dry

December

Cool and dry. Keep frost protection ready for tropicals. Keep pruning on tropicals light, and use the slow season for wiring conifers and planning next year's work.

Hints and tips

Things I wish I'd known

01

Turn your trees a quarter turn every week or two so they grow evenly toward the light.

02

Water twice: a quick pass to wet the soil, then a full soak a minute later. Dry soil sheds the first pass.

03

A wooden chopstick pushed into the soil is the cheapest moisture meter you'll ever own.

04

Take a photo from the same spot every month. Progress is too slow to see any other way.

05

Seal big cuts on ficus and most tropicals with cut paste; small ones heal on their own. Jades are the exception: leave their cuts open (see the care guide).

06

Don't fertilize a sick or freshly repotted tree. Fix the cause first.

07

Raise pots off the ground on a bench or block so roots don't grow out the bottom and pests stay off.

08

Buy the best nursery stock you can find with good roots and a thick trunk. Branches are easy to grow; trunks take years.

09

Join a local club. You'll learn more in one workshop than in a month of videos.

10

Patience is the whole game. Most mistakes come from doing too much, too soon.

FAQ

Questions people ask

Is bonsai hard for beginners?

Not if you start with the right tree. A ficus, Portulacaria or bougainvillea forgives most mistakes and loves Florida. The hard part is patience, not skill.

How long does it take to make a bonsai?

A nice-looking tree can come together in two or three years. A refined one takes many more. That's half the fun: the tree is never finished, and neither are you.

What do I need to get started?

A healthy tree with a good trunk, a fast-draining soil, a concave cutter, sharp shears and some aluminum wire. Add more tools only when you know you need them.

Can I keep a bonsai indoors?

In Florida, keep it outside. Even tropicals want real sun and fresh air, and they struggle on a windowsill. Bring them in only on the rare cold night.

How often should I water?

Check every day and water thoroughly when the top of the soil starts to dry. In the hot months that can mean daily or more. There's no fixed schedule; the tree and the weather decide.

Do you charge for advice?

No. Questions are always free. If I can help you on your journey, that's the point of all this.
